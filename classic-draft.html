Case studies

Case study 01

Daily field reports: from handwritten notes to verified AI drafts

At the construction contractor where I work, daily reports used to be paper: handwritten notes from the field, retyped and reconciled by hand. I built an AI-assisted workflow that turns crew notes and jobsite photos into structured draft reports — and then checks its own output. The workflow compares dates, crews, equipment, activities, and omissions against the source material, flags inconsistencies, and only then hands the draft to a person for approval.

  • Nothing goes out unreviewed: the human approval step is part of the design, not an afterthought.
  • Every draft is traceable back to the notes and photos it came from.
  • I taught the field and office staff the new process in person, and adjusted it around how they actually work — that's why it stuck.

Case study 02

An always-on AI operations system I design and direct

I run a personal operations system built around Claude and other models: scheduled agents that produce daily summaries and documentation, a live dashboard of current work, a shared knowledge base the agents read and write, and written rules they follow — including hard approval gates before anything touches the outside world (email, publishing, purchases: a human says yes first).

  • When something fails, the failure and the fix get written down the same day. The system is documented well enough to survive without me watching it.
  • I write the rules, prompts, and tests, and review the output; the code is built with AI coding agents working under my direction.
  • It runs across multiple machines — a Mac Studio for local models and heavy jobs, a small always-on server for scheduled work.

Case study 03

An automated job-application pipeline (this search, automated)

Most recently I built the system that's running my own job search: a form-filling engine that tailors a one-page resume per role, fills ATS applications from a verified answer bank, screenshots everything for a proof ledger, and parks any question it can't answer truthfully instead of guessing. A local model handles fuzzy matching so the marginal cost per application is close to zero — and human-verification steps stay human, on purpose.

  • Deterministic code does ~95% of the work; a local Qwen model arbitrates unclear form fields; every unknown field it hits becomes a new rule.
  • First day live it submitted more applications than the previous six weeks of manual effort combined.
  • A public, privacy-safe edition of the underlying review system is on GitHub.
